    DefinitionCounterclockwise convex polygons#✓fully proved

    EuclideanGeometry.IsCcwConvexPolygon

    Let \(P\) be an oriented two-dimensional Euclidean affine space. Points \(p_0, \dots, p_{n-1} \in P\) form a counterclockwise convex polygon if for all indices \(i < j < k\) the oriented angle \(\measuredangle p_i p_j p_k\) at \(p_j\) has sign \(-1\), that is, \(p_i, p_j, p_k\) are not collinear and lie in counterclockwise order with respect to the orientation of \(P\). Every list of at most two points qualifies.

    def IsCcwConvexPolygon (p : Fin n → P) : Prop :=
  ∀ ⦃i j k⦄, i < j → j < k → (∡ (p i) (p j) (p k)).sign = -1

    DefinitionConvex polygons#✓fully proved

    EuclideanGeometry.IsConvexPolygon

    Points \(p_0, \dots, p_{n-1}\) of an oriented two-dimensional Euclidean affine space form a convex polygon if one of the lists \(p_0, p_1, \dots, p_{n-1}\) and \(p_0, p_{n-1}, p_{n-2}, \dots, p_1\) is a counterclockwise convex polygon: for any three of its entries \(q, r, s\), in the order in which they occur in that list, the oriented angle \(\measuredangle q r s\) at \(r\) has sign \(-1\). Thus for \(n \ge 3\) the points are the vertices of a strictly convex polygon, listed counterclockwise or clockwise starting from \(p_0\); every list of at most two points qualifies.

    def IsConvexPolygon {n : ℕ} (p : Fin n → P) : Prop :=
  IsCcwConvexPolygon p ∨ IsCcwConvexPolygon fun i => p (-i)

    EuclideanGeometry.orientedTriangleArea

    Let \(\omega\) be the area form of the given orientation of the Euclidean plane, so that \(\omega(u, v)\) is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the vectors \(u\) and \(v\). For points \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) of the plane, the oriented area of the triangle with vertices \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) is the real number \(\omega(a - c, b - c) / 2\).

    DefinitionRight-angled triangles#✓fully proved

    EuclideanGeometry.IsRightAngled

    Points \(a, b, c\) of a Euclidean affine space are the vertices of a right-angled triangle if they are pairwise distinct and the unoriented angle at one of the three vertices equals \(\pi / 2\): \(\angle bac = \pi / 2\), \(\angle abc = \pi / 2\) or \(\angle bca = \pi / 2\).

    The distinctness hypothesis rules out degenerate triples, whose angles can coincide with \(\pi / 2\).

    def IsRightAngled (a b c : P) : Prop :=
  a ≠ b ∧ a ≠ c ∧ b ≠ c ∧
    (∠ b a c = π / 2 ∨ ∠ a b c = π / 2 ∨ ∠ b c a = π / 2)

    DefinitionIsosceles trapezoids#✓fully proved

    EuclideanGeometry.IsIsoscelesTrapezoid

    Points \(a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}^2\) are the vertices, in counterclockwise order, of an isosceles trapezoid if they are in strictly convex position and listed counterclockwise (for any three of them \(p, q, r\), in the order in which they occur in the list \(a, b, c, d\), the oriented angle \(\measuredangle p q r\) at \(q\) has sign \(-1\)), the line through \(a\) and \(b\) is parallel to the line through \(c\) and \(d\) (the two bases), and the diagonals have equal length: \(\operatorname{dist}(a, c) = \operatorname{dist}(b, d)\).

    One pair of parallel sides together with equal diagonals is the classical characterization of an isosceles trapezoid; in particular it rules out non-rectangular parallelograms.

    def IsIsoscelesTrapezoid (a b c d : ℝ²) : Prop :=
  IsCcwConvexPolygon ![a, b, c, d] ∧
  (affineSpan ℝ {a, b}).Parallel (affineSpan ℝ {c, d}) ∧
  dist a c = dist b d

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.ConvexPositionHigherDimensionsErdosProblem651Wanted.convexPositionThresholdExponential_refuted

    For natural numbers \(k\) and \(n\) let \(f_k(n)\) be the least \(m\) such that every set of \(m\) points of \(\mathbb{R}^k\) in general position (any at most \(k + 1\) of them affinely independent) contains \(n\) points in convex position (none of them in the convex hull of the others), with \(f_k(n) = 0\) if there is no such \(m\). It is not the case that for every \(k \ge 2\) there is a real \(c > 0\) with \((1 + c)^n < f_k(n)\) for all sufficiently large \(n\).

    Pohoata and Zakharov proved \(f_d(n) = 2^{o(n)}\) for every \(d \ge 3\), so for \(k = 3\) and every \(c > 0\) the bound \(f_3(n) \le (1 + c)^n\) holds for all large \(n\); erdosproblems.com/651 lists the problem as disproved.
