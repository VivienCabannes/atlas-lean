    Viscosity solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi equations

    Crandall and Lions introduced viscosity solutions (1983) to give first-order Hamilton–Jacobi equations \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) a theory with existence, uniqueness and stability for merely continuous solutions. A function is a subsolution when every \(C^1\) test function \(\varphi\) touching it from above at \(x\) satisfies \(F(x, u(x), D\varphi(x)) \le 0\), and a supersolution when every test function touching from below satisfies \(F \ge 0\). The notion is consistent with classical solutions, is not symmetric in the sign of \(F\) (\(-|x|\) solves \((u')^2 - 1 = 0\), \(|x|\) does not), is stable under locally uniform limits, and admits a comparison principle, proved by doubling the variables, that yields uniqueness. For convex superlinear Hamiltonians the Hopf–Lax formula gives the solution of the Cauchy problem \(u_t + H(Du) = 0\) explicitly.

    Here the definitions, consistency, the eikonal example of the user's guide, stability under locally uniform limits, and the comparison principle for continuous sub- and supersolutions (in the model case \(\gamma u + H(Du) = f(x)\) and under the general structure conditions of the user's guide) are proved; comparison for semicontinuous sub- and supersolutions and the Hopf–Lax formula are recorded as wanted. Perron's method, second-order equations (the theorem on sums) and half-relaxed limits are not formalized.

    DefinitionViscosity subsolution of a first-order equation#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.IsViscositySubsolution

    Let \(\Omega\) be a subset of a real normed space \(E\) and \(F : E \times \mathbb{R} \times E^* \to \mathbb{R}\). A function \(u\) is a viscosity subsolution of \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) on \(\Omega\) if \(u\) is upper semicontinuous on \(\Omega\) and, whenever \(\varphi\) is a \(C^1\) function and \(u - \varphi\) has a local maximum relative to \(\Omega\) at \(x \in \Omega\), $\(F(x, u(x), D\varphi(x)) \le 0 .\)$ The test function "touches \(u\) from above" at \(x\), and the inequality is what a classical subsolution would satisfy there, since at an interior maximum of \(u - \varphi\) the two gradients agree (Crandall–Ishii–Lions, (2.1)–(2.2)). The gradient is a continuous linear functional, so no inner product is needed.

    The user's guide defines the notion through second-order superjets and notes in Remarks 2.3 that it is equivalent to the test-function form; Calder uses \(C^\infty\) test functions and remarks (Remark 2.7) that \(C^1\) test functions give the same notion. The formalization uses \(C^1\) test functions.

    The test functions are \(C^1\) on the whole space \(E\). In finite dimensions, the setting of every theorem here that needs more than the definition, this is the same as testing with functions that are \(C^1\) near \(x\), since multiplying by a smooth bump function equal to \(1\) near \(x\) changes neither the local maximum nor \(D\varphi(x)\).

    def IsViscositySubsolution (F : E → ℝ → (E →L[ℝ] ℝ) → ℝ) (Ω : Set E) (u : E → ℝ) : Prop :=
  UpperSemicontinuousOn u Ω ∧
    ∀ x ∈ Ω, ∀ φ : E → ℝ, ContDiff ℝ 1 φ → IsLocalMaxOn (fun y => u y - φ y) Ω x →
      F x (u x) (fderiv ℝ φ x) ≤ 0

    DefinitionViscosity supersolution of a first-order equation#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.IsViscositySupersolution

    A function \(u\) is a viscosity supersolution of \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) on \(\Omega\) if \(u\) is lower semicontinuous on \(\Omega\) and, whenever \(\varphi\) is a \(C^1\) function and \(u - \varphi\) has a local minimum relative to \(\Omega\) at \(x \in \Omega\), $\(F(x, u(x), D\varphi(x)) \ge 0 .\)$ This is the mirror image of a viscosity subsolution, with test functions touching from below. The two notions are not exchanged by replacing \(F\) with \(-F\): see the eikonal example.

  LowerSemicontinuousOn u Ω ∧
    ∀ x ∈ Ω, ∀ φ : E → ℝ, ContDiff ℝ 1 φ → IsLocalMinOn (fun y => u y - φ y) Ω x →
      0 ≤ F x (u x) (fderiv ℝ φ x)

    DefinitionViscosity solution of a first-order equation#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.IsViscositySolution

    A viscosity solution of \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) on \(\Omega\) is a function that is both a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution on \(\Omega\). Being upper and lower semicontinuous, it is continuous on \(\Omega\). The name comes from the method of vanishing viscosity, which produces such solutions as limits of solutions of \(F(x, u, Du) = \varepsilon \Delta u\); the theory itself makes no use of it (Crandall–Ishii–Lions, Remarks 2.3).

    def IsViscositySolution (F : E → ℝ → (E →L[ℝ] ℝ) → ℝ) (Ω : Set E) (u : E → ℝ) : Prop :=
  IsViscositySubsolution F Ω u ∧ IsViscositySupersolution F Ω u

    TheoremClassical subsolutions are viscosity subsolutions#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.isViscositySubsolution_of_classical

    Let \(\Omega\) be open. If \(u\) is differentiable at every point of \(\Omega\) and \(F(x, u(x), Du(x)) \le 0\) for all \(x \in \Omega\), then \(u\) is a viscosity subsolution of \(F = 0\) on \(\Omega\).

    At an interior local maximum of \(u - \varphi\) the derivative of \(u - \varphi\) vanishes, so \(D\varphi(x) = Du(x)\) and the viscosity inequality is the classical one. In the second-order theory degenerate ellipticity enters here; at first order nothing else is needed.

    TheoremConsistency of viscosity and classical solutions#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.isViscositySolution_iff_of_contDiff

    On an open set \(\Omega\), a \(C^1\) function is a viscosity solution of \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) if and only if \(F(x, u(x), Du(x)) = 0\) for every \(x \in \Omega\). One direction is classical solutions are viscosity solutions; the other tests with \(\varphi = u\) (subsolution, supersolution). So the viscosity notion extends, and does not change, the classical one.

    IsViscositySolution F Ω u ↔ ∀ x ∈ Ω, F x (u x) (fderiv ℝ u x) = 0

    Theorem\(-|x|\) is a viscosity solution of \((u')^2 - 1 = 0\) on \(\mathbb{R}\)#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.isViscositySolution_neg_abs

    The function \(u(x) = -|x|\) is a viscosity solution of \((u')^2 - 1 = 0\) on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), kink included. Away from \(0\) it is a classical solution. At \(0\), every \(C^1\) function touching \(u\) from above satisfies \(\varphi(t) - \varphi(0) \ge -|t|\) near \(0\), so its slope lies in \([-1, 1]\) and \(\varphi'(0)^2 - 1 \le 0\); and no \(C^1\) function touches \(u\) from below at \(0\), so the supersolution condition is empty there.

    By contrast \(|x|\) is not a viscosity supersolution. Since \(|x| = -(-|x|)\), a viscosity solution of \(F = 0\) need not give one of \(-F = 0\) after a change of sign: the theory is one-sided, which is what makes it select the physically relevant solution.

    theorem isViscositySolution_neg_abs : IsViscositySolution eikonal univ (fun x => -|x|)

    TheoremStability of viscosity subsolutions under locally uniform limits#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Analysis.ViscositySolution.IsViscositySubsolution.of_tendstoLocallyUniformlyOn

    Let \(F\) be jointly continuous and \(\Omega\) open in a finite-dimensional real inner product space. If \(u_n\) are continuous viscosity subsolutions of \(F(x, u, Du) = 0\) on \(\Omega\) and \(u_n \to U\) locally uniformly on \(\Omega\), then \(U\) is a viscosity subsolution on \(\Omega\).

    Given a \(C^1\) function \(\varphi\) with \(U - \varphi\) maximal at \(x_0\), replace \(\varphi\) by \(\psi = \varphi + |x - x_0|^2\), which makes the maximum strict without changing \(D\varphi(x_0)\). Maximisers \(x_n\) of \(u_n - \psi\) on a small closed ball converge to \(x_0\), hence are interior for large \(n\), so \(F(x_n, u_n(x_n), D\psi(x_n)) \le 0\); continuity of \(F\) and of \(D\psi\) passes this to the limit.     Background
    • Fenchel conjugate: \(L\) is the convex conjugate of \(H\), and \(H = L^*\) by the Fenchel–Moreau theorem; here both are real valued, so the statement writes the supremum directly.
    • Linear quadratic regulator: dynamic programming in discrete time, where the Riccati recursion plays the role of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for quadratic value functions.
