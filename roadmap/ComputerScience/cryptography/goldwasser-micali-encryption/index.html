    Goldwasser–Micali encryption

    The first public-key encryption scheme proved to hide all partial information about the plaintext (Goldwasser and Micali, STOC 1982). Let \(N = pq\) be a product of two distinct primes and let \(y\) be a quadratic non-residue modulo \(N\) with Jacobi symbol \(+1\). The public key is \((N, y)\) and the secret key is \((p, q)\). A bit \(b\) is encrypted, with a fresh uniform unit \(x\) modulo \(N\), as \(x^2\) if \(b = 0\) and as \(y x^2\) if \(b = 1\); a string is encrypted bit by bit. With the factorization, decryption tests quadratic residuosity by Euler's criterion modulo \(p\) and modulo \(q\). Without it, deciding whether a number with Jacobi symbol \(+1\) is a square modulo \(N\) is the quadratic residuosity problem.

    Here the scheme is formalized for a fixed modulus \(N\). Correctness rests on Goldwasser and Micali's Lemma 2 (Euler's criterion modulo \(p\) and \(q\), glued by the Chinese remainder theorem). The security theorems are per adversary: an eavesdropper with advantage \(\varepsilon\) against one-bit encryption gives a distinguisher with advantage \(2\varepsilon\) in the residuosity experiment (Goldwasser–Micali's main theorem for one bit), every adversary computing a function of the plaintext bit is matched by a simulator up to the residuosity advantage of one distinguisher built from it, and for messages of \(\ell\) bits the hybrid argument (their Theorem 3) bounds a distinguisher's advantage by the sum of \(\ell\) residuosity advantages. Each reduction calls the adversary once, so the statements carry over to efficient adversaries.

    In this concrete model every function counts as an adversary, and an unbounded distinguisher can decide residuosity: for any key whose \(y\) is not a square, the hypothesis "every distinguisher has residuosity advantage at most \(\varepsilon\)" forces \(\varepsilon \ge 1\), and the scheme is not indistinguishable for any error below \(1\). The all-distinguisher corollaries are kept, labelled as vacuous, and proved to be so.

    In the STOC version, the partial-information security of Section 4.1 is proved for a uniformly distributed message space and easily computable predicates; the general definition used here is Goldreich's. Not formalized: the asymptotic statements under Goldwasser and Micali's quadratic residuosity assumption on circuits, the random self-reducibility of quadratic residuosity (their Theorems 1 and 2), and the fact that \(y x^2\) is uniform among non-residues with Jacobi symbol \(+1\) (the residuosity experiment is stated with \(y x^2\) directly, and does not require \(y\) to have Jacobi symbol \(+1\)).

    TheoremGoldwasser–Micali's Lemma 2#✓fully proved

    Cslib.Crypto.PublicKey.GoldwasserMicali.isSquare_iff_euler

    Let \(p \ne q\) be primes and let \(c\) be a unit modulo \(pq\). Then \(c\) is a square modulo \(pq\) if and only if \(c^{\lfloor p/2 \rfloor} \equiv 1 \pmod p\) and \(c^{\lfloor q/2 \rfloor} \equiv 1 \pmod q\).       ((c : ZMod (p * q)).cast : ZMod q) ^ (q / 2) = 1

    Cslib.Crypto.PublicKey.GoldwasserMicali.reduction

    From an eavesdropper \(A\) against the one-bit scheme: on the public key \(y\), a challenge \(z\) and coins \(r\), let \((m_0, m_1)\) be the two messages \(A\) chooses.
