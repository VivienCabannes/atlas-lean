    Sequential tests and quickest change detection

    Observations \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) arrive one at a time and the statistician decides after each one whether to stop. To test \(P_0\) against \(P_1\) with i.i.d. observations, Wald's sequential probability ratio test stops when the log-likelihood ratio \(\Lambda_n\) leaves an interval \((a, b)\); Wald's inequalities bound its error probabilities by the boundaries, and the Wald–Wolfowitz theorem says that no test with smaller or equal error probabilities has smaller expected sample size under either hypothesis. In quickest change detection the law switches from \(P_0\) to \(P_1\) at an unknown time: with a geometric prior on the change point, Shiryaev's rule (stop when the posterior probability of a change exceeds a threshold) minimises the expected delay under a false-alarm constraint, and without a prior Page's CUSUM minimises Lorden's worst-case delay for a given mean time to false alarm (Moustakides 1986). The definitions, the closed form of CUSUM and Wald's inequalities (through the likelihood-ratio change of measure on the first \(n\) observations) are proved; the three optimality theorems are wanted statements. All of these problems reduce to optimal stopping problems (Peskir and Shiryaev, Optimal Stopping and Free-Boundary Problems, 2006, Introduction (B) and Chapter VI, where the continuous-time Wiener and Poisson versions are solved).

    TheoremDensity of a finite product of absolutely continuous laws#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.Measure.pi_eq_withDensity_rnDeriv

    If P₁ ≪ P₀ are probability measures on X and ι is finite, the product law P₁^ι has density x ↦ ∏ i, (dP₁/dP₀)(x i) with respect to P₀^ι: the likelihood ratio of |ι| independent observations, as in the integrals of Wald's inequalities (Encyclopedia of Mathematics, Sequential probability ratio test, equations (a1)–(a2)).

    The proof checks the identity on boxes \(\prod_i s_i\), where the integral of \(\prod_i (dP_1/dP_0)(x_i)\) factorises because the coordinates are independent under \(P_0^\iota\), and each factor is \(\int_{s_i} dP_1/dP_0 \, dP_0 = P_1(s_i)\).

    theorem Measure.pi_eq_withDensity_rnDeriv {ι : Type*} [Fintype ι] (P₀ P₁ : Measure X)
    [IsProbabilityMeasure P₀] [IsProbabilityMeasure P₁] (h₁₀ : P₁ ≪ P₀) :
    Measure.pi (fun _ : ι => P₁) =
      (Measure.pi fun _ : ι => P₀).withDensity (fun x => ∏ i, P₁.rnDeriv P₀ (x i))

    TheoremLikelihood-ratio change of measure on the first n observations#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.infinitePi_apply_eq_setLIntegral_prod_rnDeriv

    If P₁ ≪ P₀ and the event A depends only on the observations X₁, …, Xₙ, then P₁^ℕ(A) = ∫_A ∏_{i<n} (dP₁/dP₀)(Xᵢ) dP₀^ℕ: the likelihood ratio Rₙ is the density of P₁^ℕ with respect to P₀^ℕ on σ(X₁, …, Xₙ). This is the change of measure behind Wald's inequalities (Encyclopedia of Mathematics, Sequential probability ratio test, equation (a2)).

    The event is the preimage of a set \(B\) of the first \(n\) coordinates; the law of those coordinates under \(P_i^{\mathbb N}\) is the finite product \(P_i^n\), and \(P_1^n\) has density \(\prod_{i<n} dP_1/dP_0\) with respect to \(P_0^n\).

    theorem infinitePi_apply_eq_setLIntegral_prod_rnDeriv (P₀ P₁ : Measure X) [IsProbabilityMeasure P₀]
    [IsProbabilityMeasure P₁] (h₁₀ : P₁ ≪ P₀) (n : ℕ) {A : Set (ℕ → X)}
    (hA : MeasurableSet[observationFiltration X n] A) :
    Measure.infinitePi (fun _ : ℕ => P₁) A =
      ∫⁻ ω in A, ∏ i ∈ range n, P₁.rnDeriv P₀ (ω i) ∂Measure.infinitePi (fun _ : ℕ => P₀)

    Wald's sequential probability ratio test

    DefinitionWald's sequential probability ratio test#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.sprtTime

    With log-boundaries a < 0 < b, the SPRT stops at N = inf {n ≥ 1 : Λₙ ≤ a or Λₙ ≥ b} (⊤ if this never happens) and accepts P₁ when Λ_N ≥ b, P₀ when Λ_N ≤ a (Lai 2001, equation (2.1), with A = exp b and B = exp a).

    In Lai's notation the test stops when \(R_n = e^{\Lambda_n}\) leaves \((B, A)\) with \(A > 1 > B > 0\); here \(a = \log B\) and \(b = \log A\). The Encyclopedia of Mathematics article writes the boundaries the other way round (\(A < B\)).

    noncomputable def sprtTime (P₀ P₁ : Measure X) (a b : ℝ) : (ℕ → X) → WithTop ℕ :=
  hittingAfter (llrSum P₀ P₁) (Set.Iic a ∪ Set.Ici b) 1

    TheoremWald's inequality on the acceptance of the null hypothesis#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.sprt_acceptZero_le

    For the SPRT with log-boundaries a and b, P₁(accept P₀) ≤ e^a P₀(accept P₀), where both events require the test to stop. This is the first line of Wald's inequalities, β = ∫_{λ_N ≤ A} λ_N dP₀ ≤ A (1 - α) (Encyclopedia of Mathematics, Sequential probability ratio test, equation (a2), whose lower boundary A is e^a here); 1 - α equals P₀(accept P₀) when the SPRT stops P₀-almost surely. The source takes a < 0 < b; the inequality holds for all a and b.

    The proof splits the event according to the value \(n\) of the stopping time. On \(\{N = n,\ \Lambda_n \le a\}\), an event of the first \(n\) observations, the likelihood-ratio change of measure gives \(P_1 = \int R_n \, dP_0\) with \(R_n = e^{\Lambda_n} \le e^a\); summing over \(n\) gives the inequality. The laws \(P_0\) and \(P_1\) are assumed mutually absolutely continuous, so that \(R_n\) is positive and finite almost surely.

    theorem sprt_acceptZero_le {P₀ P₁ : Measure X} [IsProbabilityMeasure P₀]
    [IsProbabilityMeasure P₁] (h₁₀ : P₁ ≪ P₀) (h₀₁ : P₀ ≪ P₁) (a b : ℝ) :
    Measure.infinitePi (fun _ : ℕ => P₁) (sprtAcceptZero P₀ P₁ a b) ≤
      ENNReal.ofReal (Real.exp a) *
        Measure.infinitePi (fun _ : ℕ => P₀) (sprtAcceptZero P₀ P₁ a b)
    def sprtAcceptZero (P₀ P₁ : Measure X) (a b : ℝ) : Set (ℕ → X) :=
  {ω | ∃ n : ℕ, sprtTime P₀ P₁ a b ω = n ∧ llrSum P₀ P₁ n ω ≤ a}

    TheoremWald's inequality on the acceptance of the alternative#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.sprt_acceptOne_ge

    For the SPRT with log-boundaries a and b, e^b P₀(accept P₁) ≤ P₁(accept P₁): the second line of Wald's inequalities, 1 - β = ∫_{λ_N ≥ B} λ_N dP₀ ≥ B α (Encyclopedia of Mathematics, Sequential probability ratio test, equation (a2), whose upper boundary B is e^b here). With the first inequality it gives Wald's approximations e^b ≈ (1 - β)/α and e^a ≈ β/(1 - α), which are equalities when the log-likelihood ratio has no overshoot. The source takes a < 0 < b; the inequality holds for all a and b.

    TheoremWald–Wolfowitz theorem: optimality of the SPRT#●ready to prove

    MeasureTheory.sprt_wald_wolfowitz

    Let P₀ ≠ P₁ be mutually absolutely continuous and consider the SPRT with log-boundaries a < 0 < b. Let (T, D) be any test: a stopping time T of the observations with finite expected value under both hypotheses, and a rejection region D for P₀ in the stopped σ-algebra, whose error probabilities P₀(D) and P₁(Dᶜ) do not exceed those of the SPRT. Then the SPRT has smaller expected sample size under both P₀ and P₁. This is the Wald–Wolfowitz theorem (1948) as stated in Lai 2001, §2, p. 305, for non-randomised tests; randomised tests are not covered by this statement.

    The expected sample sizes are lower integrals in \([0, \infty]\), so the statement does not presuppose that the SPRT stops in finite expected time.

    DefinitionLorden's worst-case detection delay#✓fully proved

    MeasureTheory.lordenDelay

    For a stopping time T, sup_{ν ≥ 1} ess sup E_ν[(T - ν + 1)⁺ | X₁, …, X_{ν-1}] (Lorden's convention, as in Lai 2001, §3), written without conditional expectations: for a nonnegative variable Y and a σ-algebra 𝓕, ess sup E[Y | 𝓕] = sup {E[Y; F] / P(F) : F ∈ 𝓕, P(F) > 0}.

    The ess-sup form and the form used here agree: for \(Y \ge 0\) and a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F\), \(\operatorname{ess\,sup} \mathsf E[Y \mid \mathcal F] = \sup \{ \mathsf E[Y; F]/P(F) : F \in \mathcal F,\ P(F) > 0 \}\), because \(\mathsf E[Y; F] = \mathsf E[\mathsf E[Y \mid \mathcal F]; F]\). Lorden's convention counts a delay of \(1\) when the alarm is raised at the change point; Veeravalli and Banerjee drop the \(+1\) (their footnote 3), Lai keeps it.
