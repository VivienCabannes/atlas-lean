    Canonical paths and multicommodity flow

    Sinclair's geometric bounds on the spectral gap of a reversible Markov chain (A. Sinclair, Improved bounds for mixing rates of Markov chains and multicommodity flow, Combinatorics, Probability and Computing 1 (1992), 351–370). View the chain as a network in which the oriented edge (u, v) has capacity Q(u, v) = π(u) P(u, v), and route π(x) π(y) units from every state x to every other state y, along one canonical path or split over several paths (a multicommodity flow). If no edge is overloaded, the chain has no bottleneck: the congestion bounds the conductance from below (Theorem 3), and the length-weighted congestion bounds the spectral gap directly (Theorem 5 for paths, Theorem 5′ for flows; a modification of the Diaconis–Stroock bound). Both go through a Poincaré inequality, which also bounds the mixing time of the lazy chain (see the mixing-times topic). Conversely a chain that mixes in τ steps supports a flow with congestion at most 16 τ (Theorem 8; its key Lemma 7 is proved, the theorem itself is wanted), and the upper half of Cheeger's inequality for chains, λ₁ ≤ 1 - Φ²/2, is recorded as wanted. Sinclair concludes that rapid mixing, flows of polynomially bounded congestion and inverse-polynomial conductance are equivalent; here that conclusion rests on the two wanted statements, and it carries his caveats: the gap controls mixing only once the smallest eigenvalue is dealt with (for instance by making the chain lazy) and only when \(\pi(x)\) is not exponentially small.

    TheoremThe canonical-path Poincaré inequality#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Probability.CanonicalPaths.sum_sq_sub_mul_le_of_elongatedPathLoad_le

    Let \(\pi \ge 0\) be weights and \(P\) any real matrix on a finite set \(S\). Give every ordered pair \(x \ne y\) a path \(\gamma_{xy}\) from \(x\) to \(y\), and let \(B\) bound every length-weighted edge load by \(B\) times the capacity: \(\sum_{x \ne y} \pi(x)\pi(y)|\gamma_{xy}| m_{xy}(u,v) \le B\,\pi(u)P(u,v)\) for all \((u,v)\). Then for every \(\psi : S \to \mathbb{R}\),

    \[\sum_{x,y} (\psi(x)-\psi(y))^2\,\pi(x)\pi(y) \;\le\; B \sum_{x,y} (\psi(x)-\psi(y))^2\,\pi(x)P(x,y).\]

    This is the displayed computation in the proof of Theorem 5 of A. Sinclair, Improved bounds for mixing rates of Markov chains and multicommodity flow, Combinatorics, Probability and Computing 1 (1992), 351–370: write \(\psi(y)-\psi(x)\) as the telescoping sum of the increments along \(\gamma_{xy}\), apply Cauchy–Schwarz along the path (which costs the factor \(|\gamma_{xy}|\)), and regroup the sum by edges. The inequality needs neither reversibility nor stochasticity; Sinclair attributes the path technique to Diaconis and Stroock (Annals of Applied Probability 1 (1991)), whose bound weights the path length differently.

    TheoremSinclair's canonical-path bound on the spectral gap#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Probability.CanonicalPaths.one_le_mul_one_sub_of_eigenvector

    Let \(P\) be a real matrix on a finite state space \(S\) with unit row sums, reversible with respect to a probability vector \(\pi > 0\): \(\pi(x) P(x,y) = \pi(y) P(y,x)\) for all \(x, y\). Give every ordered pair of distinct states a path \(\gamma_{xy}\) from \(x\) to \(y\), and let \(B\) satisfy

    \[\sum_{x \ne y} \pi(x)\pi(y)\,|\gamma_{xy}|\, m_{xy}(u,v) \;\le\; B\, \pi(u) P(u,v) \quad\text{for every oriented edge } (u,v),\]

    where \(m_{xy}(u,v)\) counts the uses of \((u,v)\) by \(\gamma_{xy}\). Then every real eigenvalue \(\mu \ne 1\) of \(P\) satisfies \(1 \le B(1-\mu)\). When the paths use only transitions of positive probability, Sinclair's length-weighted congestion \(\bar\rho(\Gamma)\) is such a \(B\), so the spectral gap is at least \(1/\bar\rho(\Gamma)\): this is Theorem 5 of A. Sinclair, Improved bounds for mixing rates of Markov chains and multicommodity flow, Combinatorics, Probability and Computing 1 (1992), 351–370, stated there as \(\lambda_1 \le 1 - 1/\bar\rho\) for the second eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\).

    The proof follows the paper: an eigenvector \(f\) for \(\mu \ne 1\) has \(\pi\)-mean zero because \(\pi\) is stationary, so its variance and Dirichlet form are \(\sum_x \pi(x) f(x)^2\) and \((1-\mu)\sum_x \pi(x) f(x)^2\), and the canonical-path Poincaré inequality compares them. No eigenvalue need be ordered, and no spectral theorem is used; nonnegativity of \(P\) is not needed either.
