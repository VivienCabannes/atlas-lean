    Unimodal and log-concave sequences

    A finite sequence \((a_0, \dots, a_n)\) is unimodal if it weakly rises to a peak and then weakly falls, and log-concave if \(a_i^2 \ge a_{i-1} a_{i+1}\). Many sequences of combinatorics (binomial and Gaussian binomial coefficients, Stirling numbers, \(h\)-vectors, coefficients of characteristic polynomials of matroids) have these properties. For nonnegative sequences without internal zeros, log-concavity implies unimodality, and a real-rooted generating polynomial implies log-concavity (Newton). Stanley, Enumerative Combinatorics, vol. 1, Chapter 1, Exercise 50, collects the basic facts; his survey "Log-concave and unimodal sequences in algebra, combinatorics, and geometry" (1989) develops the methods.

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.Unimodal.IsUnimodal

    A finite sequence \((a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n)\) of real numbers is unimodal if for some \(0 \le j \le n\)

    \[a_0 \le a_1 \le \cdots \le a_j \ge a_{j+1} \ge a_{j+2} \ge \cdots \ge a_n.\]

    In Lean the sequence is a function \(a \colon \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}\) together with its last index \(n\); values beyond \(n\) play no role. A polynomial is called unimodal when its coefficient sequence is.

    def IsUnimodal (a : ℕ → ℝ) (n : ℕ) : Prop :=
  ∃ j ≤ n, (∀ i < j, a i ≤ a (i + 1)) ∧ ∀ i, j ≤ i → i < n → a (i + 1) ≤ a i

    DefinitionLog-concave sequence#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.Unimodal.IsLogConcave

    A finite sequence \((a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n)\) of real numbers is log-concave if

    \[a_i^2 \ge a_{i-1}\, a_{i+1} \qquad (1 \le i \le n - 1).\]

    For positive terms this says that \(\log a_i\) is a concave function of \(i\), equivalently that the ratios \(a_{i+1} / a_i\) are weakly decreasing.

    def IsLogConcave (a : ℕ → ℝ) (n : ℕ) : Prop :=
  ∀ k, k + 2 ≤ n → a k * a (k + 2) ≤ a (k + 1) ^ 2

    DefinitionSequence with no internal zeros#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.Unimodal.NoInternalZeros

    A finite sequence \((a_0, \dots, a_n)\) has no internal zeros if there do not exist \(i < j < k \le n\) with \(a_i \ne 0\), \(a_j = 0\) and \(a_k \ne 0\); that is, its nonzero terms are consecutive.

    def NoInternalZeros (a : ℕ → ℝ) (n : ℕ) : Prop :=
  ∀ i j k, i < j → j < k → k ≤ n → a i ≠ 0 → a k ≠ 0 → a j ≠ 0

    TheoremLog-concave implies unimodal#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.Unimodal.isUnimodal_of_isLogConcave

    A log-concave sequence \((a_0, \dots, a_n)\) of nonnegative real numbers with no internal zeros is unimodal, and any index of a largest term can serve as the peak.

    The hypothesis of no internal zeros cannot be dropped: \(1, 0, 0, 1\) is nonnegative and log-concave but not unimodal (a test in MathlibExtTest checks this). The proof shows that once the sequence strictly drops at some \(i\), log-concavity forces it to keep weakly decreasing, so no later term can exceed \(a_i\); symmetrically for a strict rise.

    TheoremRows of Pascal's triangle are log-concave#✓fully proved

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.Unimodal.choose_isLogConcave

    For every \(n\) the sequence \(\binom{n}{0}, \binom{n}{1}, \dots, \binom{n}{n}\) is log-concave:

    \[\binom{n}{k} \binom{n}{k+2} \le \binom{n}{k+1}^2 .\]

    Proof sketch: from \(\binom{n}{j+1}(j+1) = \binom{n}{j}(n-j)\), multiplying \(\binom{n}{k}\binom{n}{k+2}\) by \((n-k)(k+2)\) gives \(\binom{n}{k+1}^2 (k+1)(n-k-1)\), which is at most \(\binom{n}{k+1}^2 (n-k)(k+2)\). Equivalently, the ratio \(\binom{n}{k+1} / \binom{n}{k} = (n - k)/(k + 1)\) decreases with \(k\).

    Newton's theorem (Exercise 50(b)) is a different, stronger kind of statement: for a real-rooted polynomial \(\sum_i \binom{n}{i} b_i x^i\) the normalised sequence \((b_i)\) is log-concave. Together with this lemma it gives log-concavity of the coefficients of real-rooted polynomials with nonnegative coefficients.

    theorem choose_isLogConcave (n : ℕ) : IsLogConcave (fun k => (n.choose k : ℝ)) n

    MathlibExt.Combinatorics.Sequences.UnimodalProductsWanted.symmetric_unimodal_mul

    Let \(P(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{m} a_i x^i\) and \(Q(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_i x^i\) have nonnegative coefficients, and let \((a_0, \dots, a_m)\) and \((b_0, \dots, b_n)\) be symmetric (\(a_i = a_{m-i}\), \(b_i = b_{n-i}\)) and unimodal. Then the coefficients \((c_0, \dots, c_{m+n})\) of \(P(x)Q(x)\) are nonnegative, symmetric and unimodal.

    Without symmetry the product of unimodal polynomials need not be unimodal: \((1 + x + 4x^2)^2 = 1 + 2x + 9x^2 + 8x^3 + 16x^4\).

    Related topics

    • Real-rooted polynomials: real-rootedness of the generating polynomial is the strongest of the three properties and the usual way to prove log-concavity.
    • Gaussian binomial coefficients: the coefficients of \(\binom{m+n}{m}_q\) are symmetric and unimodal (Stanley, Topics in Algebraic Combinatorics, Corollary 6.10) but not log-concave in general: \(\binom{4}{2}_q = 1 + q + 2q^2 + q^3 + q^4\).
    • Neggers–Stanley conjecture: a real-rootedness conjecture for \(W\)-polynomials of posets, now refuted; real-rootedness would have implied that their coefficients are log-concave and unimodal.
